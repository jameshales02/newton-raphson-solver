Prop account risk model

Funded-trader evaluations for futures set a profit target, a trailing drawdown, a daily loss limit and often a time limit. This model simulates thousands of attempts of one strategy against those rules. It reports how often you pass, blow up or run out of time, and which position size gives the best odds.

The problem

A trader pays a fee to trade a firm's capital under strict rules. Make the profit target without touching the drawdown floor and you get a funded account; touch the floor and you start again. A strategy with a positive expectancy can still fail most attempts. The rules are path-dependent: a trailing floor follows your best day up, so a good start shrinks the room you have later. Position size decides whether you run out of time or out of drawdown. A spreadsheet of averages cannot answer "how likely am I to pass?". A simulation of whole paths can.

Rules modelled

  • Profit target \( a \) above the starting balance \( B_0 \), checked at the close, after at least the minimum number of trading days. Once the target is reached the trader stops for the day, and then completes any remaining minimum days at negligible risk, as most traders do.
  • Drawdown floor \( F_t \), initially \( B_0 - b \). Static: never moves. Trailing end-of-day: \( F_t = \max\!\big(F_{t-1},\ \min(H_t - b,\ L)\big) \), where \( H_t \) is the highest close so far and \( L \) the lock level, usually the starting balance. Trailing intraday: the same, updated after every trade. Touching the floor fails the account immediately.
  • Daily loss limit \( D \): once the day's loss reaches \( D \) the trader stops for the day, or the account fails, depending on the firm.
  • Time limit of \( T_{\max} \) trading days, after which the attempt times out.

Strategy model

Each trade per contract wins with probability \( p \) and loses otherwise. Sizes vary around the averages \( W \) and \( L_\$ \) with a lognormal multiplier of mean 1 and coefficient of variation \( c \). With probability \( \pi \) a loss is a gap or slippage loss \( m \) times larger than usual. Per contract:

\[ \mu = pW - (1-p)\,L_\$\,\big(1 + \pi(m-1)\big), \qquad \sigma^2 = pW^2(1+c^2) + (1-p)L_\$^2(1+c^2)\big(1 + \pi(m^2-1)\big) - \mu^2 . \]

With \( n \) contracts both scale: mean \( n\mu \), variance \( n^2\sigma^2 \). Alternatively, paste your own trade results and the model resamples them with replacement (an iid bootstrap). The defaults describe a modest edge: 45% winners, 1.5 to 1 reward-to-risk, \( \mu \approx \$10 \) per contract per trade against \( \sigma \approx \$143 \). That is the kind of small edge a real discretionary futures strategy might have, for example a micro E-mini (MES) trade risking 20 points to make 30.

The maths: why size matters

Strip away the daily limit, the trailing and the clock. The account is then a random walk with drift \( n\mu \) and variance \( n^2\sigma^2 \) per trade, started between an upper barrier \( a \) and a lower barrier \( b \). For Brownian motion the probability of reaching the target first is the gambler's-ruin result:

\[ P(\text{pass}) = \frac{1 - e^{-2\mu b / (n\sigma^2)}}{1 - e^{-2\mu (a+b) / (n\sigma^2)}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{trades}] = \frac{a\,P - b\,(1-P)}{n\mu}. \]

The exponent \( \mu b/(n\sigma^2) \) shrinks as size \( n \) grows. With no time limit, smaller size always gives better odds; at very large size the edge stops mattering and \( P \to b/(a+b) \), a coin toss weighted by the distances to the barriers. The time limit pushes the other way: the expected number of trades grows like \( 1/n \), so a small size runs out of days. The best size balances the two. Real trades jump past a barrier rather than touching it exactly. Siegmund's corrected diffusion approximation accounts for that overshoot by moving both barriers out by \( \rho\, n\sigma \), with \( \rho = -\zeta(\tfrac12)/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.583 \). The cross-check table on the page compares both versions with the simulation.

\[ \text{EV per attempt} = P(\text{pass}) \times V_{\text{funded}} - \text{fee}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{attempts to pass}] = 1 / P(\text{pass}). \]

  • Even with a genuine edge, the best attainable pass rate here is about 54%. A trader should expect to pay for roughly two evaluations per funded account, so the fee is a real cost of the strategy.
  • Beyond 3 contracts the odds stop improving: at that size each attempt is a near coin toss with the edge drowned out. Sizing up to "pass faster" mostly buys a faster failure.
  • The rule details matter as much as the strategy. At 2 contracts a static floor passes 65% of attempts, end-of-day trailing 54% and intraday trailing 51%, because every new high permanently removes room. Turning the daily limit into a hard fail cuts the pass rate to 48%.

Testing

  • Deterministic paths check each rule exactly: an always-winning strategy passes on the minimum day; an always-losing one hits the static floor on day 4; the daily limit stops the day or fails the account; the trailing floor rises with new highs and locks at the starting balance.
  • Ordering: intraday trailing fails at least as often as end-of-day trailing, which fails at least as often as a static floor.
  • Moments: simulated trade mean and variance match \( \mu \) and \( \sigma^2 \) above.
  • Cross-check: under the simplified rules, Monte Carlo agrees with the overshoot-corrected gambler's-ruin formula within 1.5 percentage points for 1, 2 and 4 contracts.
  • Size sweep: timeouts fall and blow-ups rise with size; bootstrap mode resamples exactly the pasted trades.

Limitations

  • Trades are independent and identically distributed. Real results cluster (tilt, regime changes, volatility), which fattens the tails and lowers pass rates. A block bootstrap of real trade history would capture some of that.
  • Trades are atomic. Intraday trailing uses closed-trade balances, but many firms trail open (unrealised) equity, which is stricter. A loss can overshoot the daily limit, whereas some platforms auto-liquidate exactly at it.
  • No consistency rules (for example, no single day above a share of total profit), no scaling plans, and no payout rules after funding. \( V_{\text{funded}} \) is a single number you supply.
  • A fixed number of trades a day and a fixed size. A trader who adapts size to the distance from the floor can do better, and that is the natural next model.
